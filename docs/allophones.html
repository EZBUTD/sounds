Sound variants: phonemes and allophones

Phoneme or allophone?

Two kinds of sound difference

A phoneme is a sound category a language uses to tell words apart. Swap one for another and the word changes: English pat and bat differ by one phoneme. Phonemes are written in slashes: /t/.

An allophone is one of the ways a single phoneme actually gets pronounced in a particular context, accent, or speaking style. Changing the allophone does not by itself change which word listeners recognize — it changes how that word sounds. Allophones are written in square brackets: [tʰ].

All three words store the same phoneme, /t/ — which is why the Sound Chart lists English /t/ once. The phonetic line on each card is what the mouth actually did, and it differs every time: [tʰ] with a puff of air in top, plain [t] in stop where the preceding s suppresses that puff, and [ɾ] as a single quick tap in butter. Substituting one allophone for another leaves the word intact, which is the standard test for whether a difference is allophonic rather than phonemic.

This is part of why two languages can light the same reference cell on the chart and still sound quite different: a shared cell means that the two selected descriptions use the same broad IPA category. It does not create one cross-language phoneme, and the phonetic realizations can differ.

Possible allophone bridges

Some sounds can be contrastive in one language and occur as an allophone in another. That can make it a useful bridge for some learners: the articulatory gesture may already be familiar.
