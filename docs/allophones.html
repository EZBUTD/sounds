Allophones and variations on sounds

Phoneme or allophone?

Two kinds of sound difference

A phoneme is a sound a language uses to tell words apart. Swap one for another and the word changes: English pat and bat differ by one phoneme. Phonemes are written in slashes: /t/.

An allophone is one of the ways a single phoneme actually gets pronounced, depending on where it sits in a word. Swapping one allophone for another doesn't change which word you've said — it changes how it sounds, in the way an accent does. Allophones are written in square brackets: [tʰ].

The phonetic line on each card is what the mouth actually did, and it differs every time: [tʰ] with a puff of air in top, plain [t] in stop where the preceding s suppresses that puff, and [ɾ] as a single quick tap in butter. Substituting one allophone for another leaves the word intact, which is the standard test for whether a difference is allophonic rather than phonemic.

This is part of why two languages can share a sound on the chart and still sound quite different: they agree on the phoneme but the allophone sounds can be different

Sounds you already make

This works in the learner's favour too. A sound can be a full phoneme in one language and merely an allophone in another, which means a speaker may already produce it without having needed to learn it. Producing a sound by habit isn't the same as controlling it deliberately, so the half weighting is a rough allowance rather than a measurement.
