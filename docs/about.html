As a Chinese-American living in Japan, I've heard the same language handled differently by different communities — and a lot of time comparing notes with other learners about what makes a language hard to pick up.

What sent me down this particular rabbit hole was a pattern in Japanese loanwords. English words arrive in Japanese and sometimes collide: food and hood can both be said as フード fūdo in Japanese. I wanted to know whether this was a coincidence or something systematic that can happen across languages (More info on the Allophones page).

Tools

Python for the data work (PHOIBLE and CLDR parsing, the acoustic analysis, the statistics), and vanilla JavaScript with canvas for the visualisations. Built with Kiro as a pair programmer; GENAI_LOG.md in the repository records what the model wrote, along with the factual errors that surfaced along the way.

The competition

Built for VizCon 2026, whose theme is “How the world lives, thrives, and connects.”

How it was built

  1. Pick one inventory per language. PHOIBLE often has several analyses of the same language, and they disagree — its Italian entries range from 30 to 70 phonemes. For each language I take the inventory whose length-collapsed count sits closest to the median of all its candidates: the “typical” analysis rather than the most maximal one. English is pinned to the Received Pronunciation inventory so its count matches the familiar 44.
  2. Match sounds at the base-symbol level. Sources differ on whether to write p or pʰ, so diacritics and length marks are stripped before comparing. This is why South Asian languages show fewer chart cells than raw phonemes — their aspirated series merges into the base cells, and the headline says so.
  3. Count consistently. Allophones aren't counted as phonemes, long and doubled forms are excluded from headline counts, and tones are counted separately and flagged. The aim is a figure comparable to the ones textbooks quote: Hindi 46, English 44.
  4. Compare every pair. All 561 pairs get a shared count and a size-normalised overlap score, plus a rarity-weighted version where sharing a click counts for more than sharing m.
  5. Check the claims against the data. Six test suites run the real page code and assert the figures quoted in the prose, so a finding that stops being true fails a test. One of them exists specifically because an earlier draft claimed “no correlation” where the data shows a moderate one.

What this can't tell you

  • Linguists disagree about phoneme inventories. Every count reflects one analysis among several. PHOIBLE documents why its sources differ; the numbers here will not match every textbook.
  • 2,177 languages is a sample, not a census, and documentation is geographically uneven. Global rarity percentages carry real uncertainty.
  • 34 languages is a small, deliberately skewed roster — mostly the world's largest languages, plus Zulu for its clicks. Findings describe this roster, not humanity.
  • Country shading is a floor, not a census. Unshaded means undocumented in these sources, not absent.
  • Sound difficulty is one part of language difficulty. Grammar, writing system and available material often matter more. Cantonese requires one new sound from English and is still widely found difficult.
  • These are correlations, not causes
  • The microphone tool does not score your pronunciation, deliberately. A single similarity number between a live microphone signal and a reference recording is dominated by pitch, vocal tract length, microphone and room rather than by how you articulated the sound; it would look precise and measure the wrong thing. F1 and F2 are shown because each corresponds to a checkable articulatory fact, but the estimator is a simple spectral peak-picker rather than the LPC analysis phonetics software uses, so on a high-pitched voice a harmonic can be misread as a formant.
