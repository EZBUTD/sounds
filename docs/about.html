As a Chinese-American living in Japan, I've heard the same language handled differently by different communities — and spent a lot of time comparing notes with other learners about what makes a language hard to pick up.

What sent me down this particular rabbit hole was a pattern in Japanese loanwords. English words arrive in Japanese and are reshaped to fit a different sound system, so they can sometimes collide: food and hood can both be said as フード fūdo in Japanese. I wanted to learn more about how systematic changes like this happen across languages.

Tools

Python for the data work (PHOIBLE and CLDR parsing, the acoustic analysis, the statistics), and vanilla JavaScript with canvas for the visualisations. Built with Kiro as a pair programmer; GENAI_LOG.md in the repository records what the model wrote, along with the errors that surfaced along the way.

How it was built

  1. Pick one source per language. PHOIBLE often has several descriptions of a language, and they disagree. Most selections follow a repeatable rule: prefer a source with usable pronunciation-variant information, then choose the broad count closest to the middle of that language's available sources. This is a practical selection rule, not a claim that the chosen accent or analysis is the single “typical” one. English is manually set to a Received Pronunciation source.
  2. Compare broad sound areas. Each occupied IPA reference area counts once. English pʰ and Spanish p can therefore share the broad p area. If a source records several distinctions in that area, the tooltip keeps those labels as useful detail, but the overlap score does not guess that they match another language's distinctions.
  3. Keep the comparison deliberately broad. Pronunciation variants are shown but are not added as separate areas. Long and doubled forms are folded into the same consonant or vowel quality, and tone is shown separately. Length and tone can absolutely distinguish words; leaving them out defines the scope of this visual rather than declaring them unimportant.
  4. Compare every pair. All 561 pairs get a shared-area count and a size-normalised overlap score. The Sound Chart shows the five highest and five lowest pairs to make the range tangible without turning it into a claim about identical pronunciation.
  5. Check that the pages agree. An automated consistency test verifies that the generated pair values, the Sound Chart and the directional learner counts all use the same occupied-area rule. Syntax and local-link checks catch broken scripts and missing assets.

What this can't tell you

  • Linguists disagree about phoneme inventories. Every count reflects one analysis among several. PHOIBLE documents why its sources differ; the numbers here will not match every textbook.
  • A broad category match is not phonetic identity. Two languages can organize or pronounce the same reference category differently. Counting an occupied area once also cannot determine that two language-specific phonemes are functionally identical. It is an approximate comparison intended to reveal interesting places to listen, with the original labels retained in the tooltips.
  • The global rarity layer is a sample, not a census. It takes one PHOIBLE inventory per Glottocode, producing 2,177 sampled inventories. Documentation is geographically uneven, so percentages should be read as “rarely recorded in this sample,” not exact facts about all languages.
  • 34 languages is a small, deliberately skewed roster — mostly the world's largest languages, plus Zulu for its clicks. Findings describe this roster, not humanity.
  • Country shading is a floor, not a census. Unshaded means undocumented in these sources, not absent.
  • Broad sound overlap is only one part of language learning. Tone, rhythm, sound combinations, grammar, writing and access to teachers can matter more. A high overlap is not a promise that a language will feel easy.
  • These are correlations, not causes.
  • The microphone tool does not score your pronunciation, deliberately. A single similarity number between a live microphone signal and a reference recording is dominated by pitch, vocal tract length, microphone and room rather than by how you articulated the sound; it would look precise and measure the wrong thing. The two displayed resonances loosely relate to mouth opening and tongue frontness, but the tool uses a simple spectral estimate rather than laboratory phonetics software. Treat the moving point as an exploration aid, not a direct picture of the tongue.
