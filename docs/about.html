About & sources

What this is, where every number came from, what it can't tell you, and why I went looking in the first place.

Why I made this

ɸ

As a Chinese-American living in Japan, I've spent a lot of time hearing the same language handled differently by different communities — and a lot of time comparing notes with other learners about what makes a language hard to pick up. The struggles people describe rarely match what a textbook says should be difficult.

What actually sent me down this particular rabbit hole was a pattern in Japanese loanwords. English words arrive in Japanese and sometimes collide: food and hood both become フード fūdo. Once you notice one, you start hearing them everywhere. I wanted to know whether that was a handful of coincidences or something systematic — and if it was systematic, whether the same thing happens in every other language too.

That question is what this project grew out of, even though the answer isn't on it. A page that tried to predict those collisions across all 34 languages was built and then cut, because the model was wrong in ways that only showed up when a Japanese reader looked at its output. The single rule behind food/hood is still here, on the Allophones page, where it belongs: one documented fact rather than eleven thousand predictions.

The competition

Built for VizCon 2026, whose theme is “How the world lives, thrives, and connects.” The brief asks for an “I had no idea” moment from public, citable data.

My candidate for that moment: language family turns out to be a fairly weak predictor of how a language sounds. Among the 34 languages here, English's closest sound-relative is Hindi rather than German, and the closest pair in the dataset is Amharic and Indonesian — two languages with no common ancestor in recorded history. I've tried to keep the conclusion that follows a practical one rather than only a surprising one: sound distance is a reasonable guide to where the pronunciation work will be, not to whether a language is within reach.

How it was built

  1. Pick one inventory per language. PHOIBLE often has several analyses of the same language, and they disagree — its Italian entries range from 30 to 70 phonemes. For each language I take the inventory whose length-collapsed count sits closest to the median of all its candidates: the “typical” analysis rather than the most maximal one. English is pinned to the Received Pronunciation inventory so its count matches the familiar 44.
  2. Match sounds at the base-symbol level. Sources differ on whether to write p or pʰ, so diacritics and length marks are stripped before comparing. This is why South Asian languages show fewer chart cells than raw phonemes — their aspirated series merges into the base cells, and the headline says so.
  3. Count consistently. Allophones aren't counted as phonemes, long and doubled forms are excluded from headline counts, and tones are counted separately and flagged. The aim is a figure comparable to the ones textbooks quote: Hindi 46, English 44.
  4. Compare every pair. All 561 pairs get a shared count and a size-normalised overlap score, plus a rarity-weighted version where sharing a click counts for more than sharing m.
  5. Check the claims against the data. Six test suites run the real page code and assert the figures quoted in the prose, so a finding that stops being true fails a test. One of them exists specifically because an earlier draft claimed “no correlation” where the data shows a moderate one.

What this can't tell you

Limits worth knowing if you plan to cite any of this

  • Linguists disagree about phoneme inventories. Every count reflects one analysis among several. PHOIBLE documents why its sources differ; the numbers here will not match every textbook.
  • 2,177 languages is a sample, not a census, and documentation is geographically uneven. Global rarity percentages carry real uncertainty.
  • 34 languages is a small, deliberately skewed roster — mostly the world's largest languages, plus Zulu for its clicks. Findings describe this roster, not humanity.
  • Country shading is a floor, not a census. Unshaded means undocumented in these sources, not absent.
  • Sound difficulty is one part of language difficulty. Grammar, writing system and available material often matter more. Cantonese requires one new sound from English and is still widely found difficult.
  • These are correlations, not causes, the lingua-franca finding included.
  • The microphone tool does not score your pronunciation, deliberately. A single similarity number between a live microphone signal and a reference recording is dominated by pitch, vocal tract length, microphone and room rather than by how you articulated the sound; it would look precise and measure the wrong thing. F1 and F2 are shown because each corresponds to a checkable articulatory fact, but the estimator is a simple spectral peak-picker rather than the LPC analysis phonetics software uses, so on a high-pitched voice a harmonic can be misread as a formant.
  • Two spectrograms are two recordings, not a language. top and stop show what one speaker did once; the aspiration pattern they illustrate is general, the exact millisecond values are not.

What got cut, and why

A page that predicted loanword collisions, removed after a reader checked it

A page called Lost in Translation used to sit here. It generalised the food/hood observation into a model: map each English phoneme to the closest sound a target language has, apply that to ~11,800 common English words, and report which pairs collapse into a homophone. It passed a validation suite of nine documented mergers and reported a collision rate per language.

It was wrong, and the way it was wrong is instructive. A Japanese speaker looked at its example cards and pointed out that most of the words it produced were not Japanese. Tracing them back found four separate defects:

  • Vowel length was discarded. The shared symbol-normalising step strips length marks, so English fast /fɑːst/ and first /fɜːst/ both became the same string. Japanese length is phonemic — ファスト and ファースト are different words — so the model invented a merger out of the exact contrast it had thrown away. This inflated every collision rate, not just the examples.
  • Half the examples relied on a dialect variant. The lexicon holds several pronunciations per word and weighted them equally, so a single regional or archaic variant could manufacture a collision. Four of the eight Japanese examples disappeared when restricted to each word's primary pronunciation.
  • A pinned substitution overreached. English /f/ → /h/ was pinned to set up the seed case, since Japanese /h/ is [ɸ] before /u/. But the context rule only fired before /u/, so every other /f/ stayed /h/ and fast came out as ハスト instead of ファスト.
  • A fix was written at the wrong layer. The transliterator had a rule spelling /ŋ/ as ング, added specifically because ン gave the wrong word for long. The model had already rewritten /ŋ/ upstream, so the rule could never fire and the page still showed the wrong form.

The uncomfortable part is that the transliterator had its own passing test suite, including food → フード. That test fed in hand-written phoneme strings the pipeline never actually produces; run end to end, the real pipeline turned food into フド. The page's own headline example was not reproduced by the code generating the page, and every test was green.

The defects were fixable. The reason for removing the page rather than fixing it is that predicted collisions have no external ground truth to check against — the nine-merger suite was the only anchor, and passing it turned out to be compatible with being badly wrong everywhere else. That is the same conclusion reached earlier about a cross-language allophone divergence metric, which was cut for the same reason. Where a claim can't be validated against something outside the model, that is a reason not to publish it, not a reason to add a caveat.

What survives is the one part that was never a prediction: Japanese /h/ → [ɸ] before /u/, giving food = hood. It's a documented fact from reference grammars, it's on the Allophones page, and it comes with audio you can check by ear.

Tools

Python for the data work (PHOIBLE and CLDR parsing, the acoustic analysis, the statistics), and vanilla JavaScript with canvas for the visualisations rather than a chart library. Built with Kiro as a pair programmer; GENAI_LOG.md in the repository records what the model wrote, along with the factual errors that surfaced along the way and how they were found — which is the more useful half.
