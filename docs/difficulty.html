Language learners

Sound-system overlap is symmetric: a pair has the same matching IPA sound labels in either direction. The sounds a learner may need to learn are not symmetric — they depend on the learner's first language. This page looks at that asymmetry, and then at whether unusual sounds correlate with how many people take a language up.

A note on what "unusual" means here. The charts below count sounds and score how globally rare they are. Neither is a measure of difficulty, since a rare sound is not automatically hard to learn.

The two directions differ

The same pair of languages, two different amounts to learn

Sound count is a small part of difficulty. This counts individual sounds only — not tone, rhythm, or syllable structure. For example, English speakers usually find Cantonese tones hard to learn, which is not captured in this chart.

Do unusual sounds deter learners?

We do not see this in the data

If unusual sounds put learners off, the languages with the rarest inventories would have the fewest learners. Taken at face value the data points the other way — — which would suggest languages with rarer sounds attract more learners. That reading doesn't hold up, and it's worth walking through why.

Rarity is closely tied to inventory size (r = ): a language with more sounds will tend to include more unusual ones, so the rarity score here partly reflects how many sounds a language has. Holding inventory size constant, the relationship largely disappears (r = ). One non-phonetic variable does track learner numbers more closely: whether a language serves as a regional lingua franca (r = ), which shows little relationship to how rare its sounds are (r = ). Lingua francas in this set have a median of 77M learners; the others, 8M.

From this exploration, it seems it isn't that unusual sounds attract learners, but that how unusual a language's sounds are doesn't appear to predict learner numbers in this sample, while historical and institutional factors — trade, governance, schooling — line up with it more closely.

Small sample, blunt instrument. Only 25 of these languages have a published learner estimate, and that set skews toward large, politically prominent languages — exactly where lingua-franca status is common. "Lingua franca" is hand-coded as yes/no, which flattens a continuum. These numbers describe this roster, not the world, and none of them establish causation.

What the rarity axis can't see: tone

A whole layer of contrast sits outside every number on this site

Every figure here counts segments — consonants and vowels. Pitch is a separate system, and it is excluded throughout. PHOIBLE records standalone tone entries as their own category, and this project drops those rows before computing anything because the chart grid has no axis for pitch. Phoneme counts, overlap percentages and the rarity axis above are therefore all segmental-only figures.

That matters most for exactly the languages where pitch does the most work.

Why not just add tone to the counts? Tone inventories are not commensurable with segment inventories — Cantonese's six tones combine with syllables rather than sitting alongside consonants, so adding "+6 sounds" would misdescribe the system. And PHOIBLE's tone coverage is thinner and less consistent than its segment coverage: Rather than build a tone metric on that footing, this site measures segments and states the boundary.

The takeaway

What to make of an unfamiliar sound system

English and Spanish have one of the lower sound-system overlaps in this dataset, and Spanish is also the language English speakers study most — a useful check on how much weight sound distance deserves. It's a real factor, and largely what an accent consists of, but vocabulary, grammar, writing system, shared loanwords and regular exposure all contribute, and none of them are on this chart. Unfamiliar sounds are learnable with practice, and people work through much larger gaps than these routinely. A wide gap here is a reasonable guide to where the pronunciation effort will go, not a measure of how hard the language will be overall.
