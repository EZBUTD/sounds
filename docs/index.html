Sound Chart: Phonemes

A look at the raw materials of human speech: which sounds languages use, what some languages have in common, and how that affects learners.

Before the charts

More than 7,000 languages are spoken around the world. The 34 languages used in this analysis are chosen from some of the world's largest, so the patterns describe this selection rather than all human speech.

What a phoneme is. Say pat, then bat. One small change at the beginning of the word changes the meaning entirely. That difference in sound is a phoneme: the smallest unit of sound that can change what a word means. English has around 44 of them. Every language has its own set, and no language uses all of the documented phonemes.

What the chart below is. Linguists needed a standard, universal system for describing these sounds, and created the International Phonetic Alphabet, where each symbol represents one sound.

Not all overlap is equal

The comparisons above count every shared phoneme equally. But sharing a sound like m says little — nearly every language has it — while sharing a click is far rarer. Re-scoring overlap so that rarer sounds carry more weight gives a different perspective on which pairs of languages are alike.

Patterns in the data

Ancestry is a weak predictor of phoneme overlap

Grouping these languages by ancestry (their language families) may give an expectation that relatives will sound alike. However, in this dataset: only of the 34 languages here have their closest sound-relative inside their own family. Related languages do share a little more overlap on average (median overlap % against % for unrelated pairs).

The sound family tree: grouping by sound instead of ancestry

This is a family tree built from sound alone. The languages are the labels down the right-hand side; every fork to their left is the point where two branches merged, drawn at the average overlap between everything on one side and everything on the other (scale along the bottom). So forks near the right join languages that have high overlap, and forks far to the left join groups that have little in common on average.

Hover any fork to see which languages it joins and at what overlap

The closest pair in this dataset is Amharic (Ethiopia) and Indonesian at 90% overlap, from different language families.

Why relatives often don't sound alike

Sound systems tend to change faster than vocabulary or grammar. English and Hindi separated roughly 6,000 years ago and today overlap more than English does with German. Because family trees are established from vocabulary and grammar, which change more slowly, a shared family tells us less about phoneme overlap than one might expect.

What this does and doesn't tell a learner

English and Spanish have one of the lower sound overlaps in this dataset, but Spanish is one of the languages English speakers study most. That combination is a useful reminder that sound overlap is one factor among several. While it can affect one's accent, other factors like vocabulary, grammar, writing system, shared loanwords and how much you use the language day to day all matter too. People learn languages with very different sound systems from their own routinely, and unfamiliar sounds are learnable with practice.

Method notes: Sound inventories from PHOIBLE 2.0 (CC BY-SA). Linguists analyse sound systems differently, so inventories vary by source (PHOIBLE explains why) and the counts here reflect one analysis per language rather than a settled figure. Audio from Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA); English diphthongs play whole words. An allophone (marked ◐) is a sound that occurs in the language but never distinguishes two words; some sources call these variants or realizations. Sounds are matched at the base-symbol level, setting aside length, aspiration and similar modifications, so two languages can share a cell here and still pronounce it noticeably differently; the Allophones page covers that. Shaded consonant cells are articulations generally judged not producible.
