How the world speaks

Speech and sounds play a large role in connecting many of us. Explore the patterns they share, the sounds that distinguish them, and how people carry those patterns across generations and borders.

Before the charts

The International Phonetic Alphabet, or IPA, is a shared set of symbols for describing speech sounds without relying on spelling. This exploration focuses on broad consonant and vowel areas. Tone and some finer distinctions are introduced separately.

More than 7,000 languages are spoken around the world. These 34 examples are mostly widely spoken languages, so the patterns describe this selection rather than all human speech.

Each button is an IPA reference sound. Click one to hear an example. The colors compare broad sound areas in two selected descriptions. Point to or focus a button to see any more detailed label used by the source.

Patterns in the data

Related languages do not always sound most alike

Grouping these languages by ancestry (their language families) reveals that in this sample, only of the 34 selected languages have their highest sound overlap inside their own family. Family history is only part of how a language sounds

The heatmap compares languages as they are described today. Related languages can grow less alike as their sounds change over generations. Languages from different families can also end up using many of the same broad sound categories, from influencing each other or by coincidence.

Connection does not require sameness

People do not need identical sound systems to understand, borrow from, or learn from one another. Languages continually reshape sounds inside words, across generations, and across borders. The rest of this project follows those changes from the mouth, through history, and into communities around the world.

Method notes: Source inventories from PHOIBLE 2.0 (CC BY-SA). Linguists analyse sound systems differently, so inventories vary by source (PHOIBLE explains why) and the counts here reflect one analysis per language rather than a settled figure. Audio from Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA); English diphthongs play whole words. The V badge means the selected source records that chart sound as a pronunciation variant (an allophone) of another category; it is not a complete claim about the language, because allophone coverage differs greatly by source. Overlap counts each occupied broad IPA area once. Thus English pʰ and Spanish p can share the broad p area. If a source records several distinctions inside one area, the tooltip preserves them as detail but the score still counts the area once. Long and doubled forms are also folded into the same broad quality for this comparison; that does not mean length is unimportant in the language. This is an invitation to explore broad patterns, not a claim that two languages share one phoneme or pronounce an area identically. The Sound Variants page covers that. Shaded consonant cells are articulations generally judged not producible.
