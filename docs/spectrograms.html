Seeing sounds

A spectrogram is a picture of sound: time runs left to right, pitch runs bottom to top, and darker means more energy at that pitch and moment. This page uses one to measure a difference the Allophones page asks you to hear — two allophones of the single phoneme /t/, the t of top against the t of stop. Then you can put your own voice on the same kind of chart.

Two allophones of one phoneme, measured

The puff of air you can see

Both words below store the same phoneme, /t/ — but the mouth produces a different sound in each. Those two sounds are allophones of /t/: alternative pronunciations that never distinguish two English words, so most English speakers hear them as one t. The Allophones page introduces the idea and lets you hear all three; this page measures two of them.

The notation is the quickest way to keep the two levels apart. /slashes/ mark a phoneme, the category the language stores. [brackets] mark an allophone, what a mouth actually did. So each card below reads /t/ → [tʰ]: one phoneme in, one allophone out.

In top the release of the [tʰ] is followed by a stretch of noisy, high-frequency energy — the aspiration — before the vowel starts. In stop that stretch is almost absent, because a preceding /s/ suppresses it, giving plain [t]. The gap between the release and the start of voicing is called voice onset time, and it is the standard way phoneticians measure aspiration. It is the one number that separates these two allophones.

Press play and a cursor tracks across the image in time with the audio, so you can hear which part of the picture you are looking at — the aspiration is the shaded stretch the cursor crosses between the two markers. Both recordings are the same speaker in the same accent, so the difference between the two pictures is the allophone rather than the voice. The markers are measured from these files, not copied from a reference table; the method and its failure modes are in analyze_spectrograms.py.

Your own voice

Where your vowel actually lands

The vowel chart on the Sound Chart page is not decorative: its two axes are measurable. A vowel's first resonance (F1) tracks how open your mouth is, and the second (F2) tracks how far forward your tongue sits. Estimating both from a microphone puts your vowel at a point on that same chart.

Hold a steady vowel — ee, ah, oo — for about a second. Your position appears as a moving dot against the reference vowels. Audio is analysed in your browser and never uploaded or stored.

input Microphone off.
There is no score here, on purpose. It is tempting to print a percentage for how closely you matched a sound, and we deliberately do not. A single similarity number over a microphone signal is dominated by your pitch, vocal tract length, microphone and room — not by how you articulated the sound. It would look precise and measure the wrong thing. F1 and F2 are shown instead because they correspond to something real and checkable: how open your mouth is and how far forward your tongue sits. Even then, treat the position as approximate. Formant estimates vary with voice and hardware, and the reference vowels are idealised chart positions rather than one speaker's measurements.
