A short history of English sounds

The Sound Chart shows every language as it is spoken now — a snapshot of something still moving. The line below is English. Sounds flow into it from the languages English met, and out of it toward the relatives that kept what English dropped.

Sounds flowing in and out

Branches above the line arrive; branches below it leave. Each carries the sound itself — click to hear it — and the words that show it, in English and in the language on the other end.

Two ways a sound arrives. An arriving branch labelled from Norman French or from French means contact : borrowed words brought the sound with them, and English kept it. A branch labelled from inside English means the opposite — no borrowing at all. The sound developed out of a sound English already had, as speakers gradually shifted how they pronounced it in certain positions, until the result counted as a separate sound. tʃ is the case here: Old English inherited a k and softened it before front vowels, so church parted ways with German Kirche and Dutch kerk, which kept the k. Same ancestor, no loanword involved. The how line on each branch says which sound it came from.

The ⟨gh⟩ in knight

Knight is spelled the way it was said. In Middle English both the ⟨k⟩ and the ⟨gh⟩ were pronounced — the ⟨k⟩ as an ordinary , the ⟨gh⟩ as , the rasp at the end of Scottish loch. English lost both and kept the letters. of the languages charted here have x; German and Dutch are among them.

Where English writes a silent ⟨gh⟩, German usually still says something. A caution on the symbol: PHOIBLE's German inventory lists a single dorsal fricative, x. German also has a fronter version in words like ich, and whether that is one sound or two has been argued for decades — this page follows the inventory it charts.
